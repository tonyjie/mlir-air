Mapping SmolVLA onto AMD XDNA NPU2

A Vision-Language-Action model on the AMD NPU, via the MLIR-AIR toolchain
Jiajie Li · 29 July 2026 · all shapes measured on the real checkpoint, all timings on real hardware (AMD Ryzen AI 9 HX 370 / NPU2 Strix AIE2P, CPU governor performance, NPU pmode=Turbo)

Jiajie Li — fifth-year PhD student at Cornell, advised by Prof. Zhiru Zhang

I joined AMD as an intern in January, working with Erwei Wang and Samuel Bayliss, and I am continuing part-time and remotely. My work is on agentic workflows that automate the deployment and optimization of LLMs onto AMD NPUs.

We built such a workflow — organised around a library of reusable skills — that takes an LLM end-to-end onto AMD XDNA NPU2 through the MLIR-AIR toolchain, at reasonable performance, in a matter of hours rather than weeks. That work was accepted at and presented to the MLArchSys'26 workshop, co-located with ISCA, and I am continuing to extend it.

This talk is the current extension: taking the same approach beyond language models to a Vision-Language-Action model — SmolVLA — on AMD XDNA2.

Part A — The SmolVLA model What the model is, how it is built, and where it spends its time on a CPU.

1. What is SmolVLA?

SmolVLA is a Vision-Language-Action model (VLA) — think of it as a robot's brain. It comes from Hugging Face's LeRobot ecosystem.

In one sentence: you tell a robot arm "pick up the cube" and give it a few camera views. SmolVLA reads the images + that sentence + the robot's current pose, and outputs a sequence of moves to make next (joint angles, gripper open/close). It fuses "see + understand + decide how to move" into one model.
Inputs / outputs (the lerobot/smolvla_base checkpoint, 450M params):
  • Inputs: 3 camera images + one natural-language instruction + a robot-state vector
  • Output: an "action chunk" — it predicts the next 50 steps of actions at once

It generates actions with a method called flow matching: starting from random noise, it iteratively "denoises" over 10 steps into a coherent set of actions — similar to how AI image generators use diffusion, except here what's being "drawn" is robot motion.

Why is the backbone a "language model"?

SmolVLA's second block is really a compact large-language model (SmolLM2-360M, same family as Llama). The difference: it doesn't "speak" (it generates no text). Instead it reads the images, text, and state all at once, understands them in a single pass, and hands an "understanding" to the action expert.

The bottleneck is vision — and the reason is that it runs three times. The model has three cameras, and the encoder is invoked once per image, serially: 182 ms each, 546 ms together, 60% of the chunk. Each image is independent work — 1024 patch tokens through 12 layers with a 1024×1024 attention — so three cameras genuinely cost three times as much (batching them saves only ~9%, measured). The language backbone, despite being the "main" model, is 8.5% because it runs once. The action expert is 31% because it runs ten times in the denoise loop. This is what tells us where acceleration would matter most.
Part B — Running it on the NPU What we moved, whether it stayed correct, how fast it is, and what is next.

B1. What runs on the NPU today — and what does not

We ported all three stages to the NPU and verified each one. We ship only one of them there. That is the interesting part of this project: the decision of what to accelerate was made by measurement, stage by stage, and two of the three stages measured slower on the NPU than on a 12-thread Zen 5.

One sentence for why: the NPU wins when the shapes are big enough to fill its 8×4 compute array, and loses when they are not. Vision has 1024 tokens and 768/3072-wide matmuls. The backbone has 256 tokens. The expert has 50.

Why the other two lose — three concrete reasons

ReasonWhat we measured
1. Small shapes cannot fill the array Same matmul kernels, three sequence lengths: seq 1024 → 3798–5790 GFLOP/s · seq 256 → 2046–3366 · seq 50 → 25–1163. The expert's 50 tokens pad to 64, and 64 admits only a tiling that occupies half the array before a single instruction runs.
2. A fixed per-launch floor Every kernel launch costs ~85 µs regardless of how little work is inside it. One denoise step of the expert issues 208 launches; a whole inference issues 2080. A 64×720 RMSNorm moves 92 KB and takes 151 µs — that is 1.2 GB/s on a fabric that reaches ~57.
3. Our FlashAttention kernel is not general enough It applies no mask and is numerically broken below 256 query tokens. The vision tower is maskless and 1024 tokens long, so it fits perfectly — this is where the vision win comes from. The backbone and the expert both need real masks, so they fall back to attention decomposed into separate matmuls plus a softmax: 11 dispatches per layer instead of 1. In the expert that alone accounts for 1760 of the 2080 dispatches and 56% of its device time.

Reason 3 is the actionable one, and it is a kernel-generality problem rather than a hardware limit. Reasons 1 and 2 are properties of the model's shapes; the only real answer to them is to accelerate a model whose shapes are bigger — see B4.

B2. Is it still correct?

Accelerating a robot policy is only interesting if it still produces the same actions. Our reference is the unmodified lerobot model running on CPU — not a reimplementation — and the comparison is the final action chunk, the (50, 6) tensor the robot would actually execute, with the noise fixed so the comparison is deterministic.

Three operations carry the whole win — and five kernels quietly give part of it back. Attention alone saves 2.9 ms of the 4.0 ms gained per layer, fc2 and fc1 another 1.9 ms. Against that, everything memory-bound runs the wrong way, and PyTorch's nn.Linear folds its bias in while the NPU launches a separate kernel for each: those five bias kernels cost 1100 µs per layer (10.5%) against a CPU counterpart of zero. Strip them and the projections stop being a wash — Q becomes 578 µs of matmul against the CPU's 693. That is the case for folding bias into the matmul epilogue.

Reading the two columns. CPU is wall-clock per nn.Module call (per-op sum = 95% of the measured stage, rest is framework dispatch); NPU is device time per kernel (sum = 94% of the deployed layer, rest is switching between its three programs; host-side costs are excluded here). Both are "the work itself" — but they are different definitions and should not be added together. Scaling up: 10.5 ms × 12 layers = 126.2 ms, versus 134.5 ms deployed; the 8.3 ms gap is that program switching.

Note the Q/K/V rows: the same 1024×768×768 matmul as fc1 and fc2 in kind, yet they lose while fc1/fc2 win. The difference is width — fc1 writes 3072 values and fc2 reduces over 3072, which fills the compute array; 768×768 does not. "Matmuls are fast on an NPU" holds only once they are big enough.

How those kernels are stacked into compiled programs

The 12 rows above are 18 kernel launches. Issuing 18 separate programs per layer would mean the host pays its per-dispatch cost 18 times. Instead we stitch them into 3 multi-launch programs, so the host pays it 3 times and every intermediate stays in device memory:

Step we tookDispatches / imageVision encoder
one program per operation (first working version)121368 ms
stitched into 3 programs per layer + on-device bias & residual38141.6 ms

2.6× from fusion alone, on the same kernels. The gain is not from removing driver overhead — it is from moving the per-Linear bias-adds and the two residual adds on-device, which eliminated a host round-trip (bf16 → f32 add → bf16) per operation. Host-side gap per image fell from 212 ms to 4 ms. A pleasant surprise: accuracy improved as a result (0.945 → 0.9906 on the encoder output), because that round-trip had been re-quantizing every intermediate.

Two lessons from this breakdown that we carry to the next model.
(a) "Matmuls are fast on an NPU" is only true once they are wide enough. Q/K/V and fc1/fc2 are the same kind of matmul, yet Q/K/V roughly tie with the CPU while fc1/fc2 win clearly — fc1 writes 3072 values and fc2 reduces over 3072, which fills the array; 768×768 does not.
(b) Anything memory-bound runs the wrong way. LayerNorm loses 5–6×, element-wise adds 2.4×. Individually small, collectively 13% of NPU time — and the five bias kernels cost 1100 µs per layer against a CPU counterpart of zero, because PyTorch folds bias into the Linear and we launch a separate kernel.

B4. What is next

Make the stages we currently keep on the CPU competitive

#WhatExpected effect
1 A more general FlashAttention kernel — accept an arbitrary mask, and work below 256 query tokens. This is the single biggest item: it is what forces both the backbone and the expert onto a decomposed attention path costing 11 dispatches per layer instead of 1. For the expert, measured components say 523 ms → ~144 ms, taking it from ~4× slower to ~1.9×
2 Fold bias and residual adds into the matmul epilogue. 13% of vision NPU time is these, and they are pure launch plus memory traffic. −52 ms est. on the vision encoder
3 Revisit fusion granularity. Fusing helped vision by 2.6×, but for the expert we measured a 1.40–1.71× device-time penalty for merging programs — the trade reverses at small shapes. −133 ms on the expert; a per-stage decision, not a global one
4 Speed up the memory-bound kernels — affine LayerNorm runs at 8.1 GB/s where a same-sized element-wise add reaches 34.7. −21 ms est.

Go after a model whose shapes suit the hardware

The clearest finding of this whole exercise is that SmolVLA is a hard case for an NPU, and specifically because it is small. Its language backbone sees 256 tokens; its action expert sees 50. The one stage with a long sequence — the 1024-token vision encoder — is the one stage that wins.

So the natural next step is a larger VLA. Models in the π0 / OpenVLA class carry a 3B-class language backbone rather than 360M, with correspondingly wider matmuls and longer sequences — exactly the regime where our measurements say the NPU pulls ahead. We already have the backbone kernels: our earlier work deployed Llama-3.2-1B/3B, Qwen3-1.7B and SmolLM2-1.7B on this hardware, and the kernel registry those runs produced is what this port reused. The expectation is that a larger VLA would move more of its stages onto the NPU and win more on each of them.

Beyond kernels: the agentic workflow itself

SmolVLA is also a test of whether the automated deployment workflow generalises past pure language models. It largely did — the registry, the fused-program builders and the verification ladder all transferred — but this model exposed three assumptions baked into shared code that no previous model had triggered: an attention block that is not square (q_dim 960 vs hidden 720), two kernels legitimately sharing a compiled object name, and a structural fact derived from a synthetic input rather than the real checkpoint. Each of those has been folded back into the skills so the next model does not rediscover them.

Summary. All three stages of SmolVLA are ported and verified on AMD XDNA NPU2. We ship the one that is faster there — the vision encoder, 1.19× per image, 1.07× end to end, action-chunk cosine 0.9990 against the unmodified CPU model. The other two stay on the CPU because they measure slower, and we know precisely why: sequences of 50 and 256 tokens cannot fill the array, and our attention kernel is not yet general enough to serve them in a single launch.
